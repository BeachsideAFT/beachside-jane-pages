Two things worth knowing before you start

The pictures are from his Mac, not yours. The screen grabs come from the teacher's computer. His scene names, his camera name and his files will not match yours. Look at where the buttons are, not at what his scenes are called.

Plain words

The words you will see

Four words come up over and over. Here is what each one means.

Scene
One whole look. Think of it as a page you have set up ahead of time — you on camera, or you beside your slides. You switch between scenes while you are live.
Overlay
Anything you put on top of the picture. A logo, a photo, your camera in a small box, a piece of text, a window from another app.
Source
What the scene starts with underneath everything. Usually your camera. You can also set it to blank, which gives you a black background to build on.
Profile
A whole set of scenes saved together. You might have one profile for a webinar and another for interviews.

Part 1

Your first scenes

A scene is a look you set up in advance. The point of building them before you go live is that you never have to fiddle with settings while people are watching. You just click.

1

Open the Scenes window

Go to the menu bar at the top of the screen and click Window. Near the bottom of that menu you will see Scenes. Click it.

The keyboard shortcut printed next to it is ⌘ \ — that is the Command key and the backslash key.

When Ecamm is brand new it gives you one scene already made, called Default Scene.

Seen on screen

2

Hide the buttons that sit over your picture

Ecamm puts small controls on top of your video picture. They are handy, but they get in the way when you are lining things up.

Click Window in the menu bar, then click Hide Main Window Controls.

The shortcut shown beside it in the menu is ⌘ `. That second key is the one directly to the left of the number 1, below the Escape key. It has a squiggle on it too.

Press it again to bring the controls back.

Worth noting: in the talk he calls this key "tilde". The menu on screen prints the other symbol that shares the same key. It is the same key either way.

Seen on screen

3

Name your plain camera scene

Start with the simplest scene of all: just you on camera, nothing on top. You will come back to this one again and again during a live.

Double-click the scene's name in the list and type a new one. In the lesson he calls his Me.

Seen on screen

4

Add another scene

Along the bottom of the Scenes window there is a row of small buttons. The first one on the left is a plus sign. Click it to add a new scene.

A fresh scene appears at the bottom of the list, ready to be named.

Name every scene as you make it. He says this twice in the lesson and it is the single best habit here. Do not leave scenes called "New Scene" or "Copy of Screen Share". When you are live you will not have time to work out which is which.

Seen on screen

5

Change the order by dragging

Scenes run top to bottom in the list. To move one, pick it up by its name and drag it up or down.

Put them in the order you will actually use them. His intro countdown goes first, then the plain camera scene.

Seen on screen

6

Make a countdown intro from a video file

Open a Finder window with your video clip in it. Drag the clip straight onto the Ecamm picture. It drops in and starts playing.

He uses a four-minute countdown before his live sessions start. It gives people time to arrive and gives him something on screen while they do.

Seen on screen

7

Use the video controller

Once a video is in a scene, Ecamm shows a small controller bar across the picture. It has a play button, buttons to skip backwards and forwards, a volume slider, and the time — how far in you are, and how long is left.

If you cannot see the controller, the main window controls are hidden. Press ⌘ ` to bring them back.

Seen on screen

8

Make the countdown move on by itself

This is the clever part. In the video controller there is a setting for what happens when the clip finishes. Set it to Go to next scene.

Now you press play on your countdown, take your hands off, and when it reaches zero Ecamm switches by itself to the next scene in the list — which is why the order in step 5 matters.

Test it before you go live

Drag the playhead in the controller to about five seconds before the end, then press play. Watch it run out and switch on its own. Better to find a problem now than on air.

Part 2

A scene for comments

When you go quiet to read a comment, viewers cannot tell what you are doing. Putting the comments on screen fixes that — they can see what you are reading. This part builds that scene from nothing.

10

Start a new scene with a blank source

Click the plus button to add a scene. Then change what the scene starts with: instead of your camera, choose Blank.

The picture goes completely black. That is correct. You are going to build on top of it.

The setting is in the box at the top left of the picture area, the one showing the current scene's name.

Seen on screen

11

Add a screen share overlay

Look at the bottom of the Overlays window. There are two rows of small buttons. The third button on the top row is the screen share button — it looks like a computer monitor.

Click it. A new row called Screen Share appears in the list, and a settings panel opens.

Seen on screen

12

Choose what to show

The settings panel has a box at the top called Show:. Click it and a list drops down. On his Mac it offers:

  • Primary Display, Secondary Display, Tertiary Display — whole screens
  • Current Application
  • Then every app that is open: ClickUp, Ecamm Live, Finder, Google Chrome, Keynote

For a comments box, pick Ecamm Live itself, then the comments window inside it. Your own list will show your own apps.

Seen on screen

13

Set the shape and turn transitions off

In the same panel, set Shape: to Custom. That lets you make the box any size and shape you like, instead of being stuck with a fixed one.

Set Transition: to None.

Below those you will also find sliders for Corner Radius (how rounded the corners are) and Border Width (how thick the edge line is), plus three tick boxes: Zoom To App Windows, Activate System Audio Capture and Green Screen Key.

Seen on screen

14

Trim the edges off with the Option key

The window you have brought in probably has parts you do not want — a grey border, a title bar, browser tabs across the top.

Move your mouse to the edge of the box. The pointer turns into a double-headed arrow.

Now hold down the Option key on your keyboard and drag that edge inwards. Instead of squashing the picture, it cuts the edge off. Do the same on each side you want trimmed.

The difference matters. Dragging an edge on its own makes the picture bigger or smaller. Holding Option while you drag cuts a slice off instead. Same handle, different result.

Seen on screen

15

Put your camera beside the comments

The scene is still blank underneath, so you are not in it yet. Add yourself as an overlay.

On the bottom row of the Overlays buttons there is a camera button. Click it and choose your camera. His is called Cam Link 4K; yours will have its own name.

Set its shape to Custom as well, so you can size it however you want.

Seen on screen

16

Line the two boxes up

Drag your camera box so it sits beside the comments. As you move it, Ecamm shows guide lines when the edges match up with something else. Let it snap.

Line up the bottoms and the outer edges. It takes a minute and it is the difference between a screen that looks made and one that looks thrown together.

There is no one right layout. You could have a small camera in a corner with big comments, or an even split. His split is roughly even.

Seen on screen

Part 3

Backgrounds

A blank scene gives you black behind everything. Black is a bit stark. You can put a colour or a pattern back there instead — and it is just an image file you drop in.

17

Make the background image first

Before you touch Ecamm, make a small image in whatever you use for design — he made his in Canva. A plain rectangle in your brand colour is enough.

Save it to your Mac somewhere you can find it.

From the talk only

18

Drop it into "Show in background"

The Overlays window is split into labelled sections. Scroll to the bottom one, headed SHOW IN BACKGROUND.

Drag your image file onto that section. It goes behind everything else in the scene, replacing the black.

Seen on screen

19

Swap it for something with texture

You can put more than one image in the background section and turn them on and off. A flat colour works; something with a light pattern gives the screen a bit more life.

Each row in the Overlays list has a small eye icon beside it. Click the eye to show or hide that item. Hide the flat one, show the patterned one.

Seen on screen

20

Round the corners and add a border

Click the pencil icon on an overlay to open its settings. Two sliders shape the box:

  • Corner Radius — how rounded the corners are. He uses 9. Go gently; a small number looks deliberate, a big one looks like a bubble.
  • Border Width — how thick the edge line is, with a colour square beside it. Click the square to pick a colour.

Use the same numbers on every box in a scene so they match.

His memory trick: he sets border width to 4 and corner radius to 20, and remembers it as "four and twenty blackbirds baked in a pie."

Seen on screen

Part 4

Copy a scene instead of starting over

Once you have one good scene, the fastest way to make the next one is to copy it and change the one thing that differs. You keep the layout, the borders and the background for free.

21

Duplicate the scene

Select the scene you want to copy. Along the bottom of the Scenes window, the second button from the left is the duplicate button — two squares overlapping.

Click it. A copy appears named Copy of whatever the original was called. Rename it straight away.

From the talk only

Partly checked. The duplicate button is visible in the button row in several shots. The moment of clicking it, and the resulting "Copy of" name, is not in any screen grab in this set.

22

Point the copy at a different app

In the copy, open the screen share settings and change Show: to the other app — slides instead of the browser, say.

The new window will not be the same shape as the old one, so the trim you did before will be wrong. Redo it with Option and drag.

Seen on screen

23

Click back into Ecamm before you resize

This one catches people out. Look at the very top of your screen: the first word in the menu bar tells you which app the Mac thinks you are using.

If you have just clicked into your slides, the menu bar says the slides app, and Ecamm's handles will not respond. Click once on the Ecamm window first. The menu bar changes back to Ecamm Live. Now the handles work.

Seen on screen

24

The rule about apps you want to share

Ecamm can only share an app that is open and sitting on your desktop. If you minimise it down to the Dock, it disappears from the list and there is nothing to share.

Before a live, open every app you plan to show and leave it open.

25

The finished screen share scene

Here is what you are aiming for: you in a small box, the website or slides filling most of the frame, both with matching borders, sitting on your background.

During the live you can scroll the shared window and your viewers see it move.

Part 5

Folders

Once you have a dozen scenes the list gets long and you start hunting. Folders group them.

26

Make a folder

The third button along the bottom of the Scenes window is the folder button. Click it.

A row called New Group appears with its name box open. Type a name.

Seen on screen

27

Drag your scenes into it

Pick each scene up by its name and drop it onto the folder. They tuck inside, and the folder gets a little arrow you click to open and close it.

One idea from the lesson: a folder for shows with one presenter and another for shows with two, since a layout built for one person does not work for two.

Seen on screen

28

Folders or profiles?

A folder groups scenes inside one profile. A profile is a whole separate set. He says he uses profiles more than folders these days — one profile per kind of show, with its own colours and fonts.

Folders are for splitting up scenes within a single show.

From the talk only

29

Two ways to work once it is all built

One scene of each. Keep one comments scene, one slides scene, and jump around them as the conversation goes.

A full run of show. For a webinar you have planned out, duplicate scenes so the list reads top to bottom in the exact order you will present. Then you only ever click the next one down. No thinking.

He also mentions using a button box — a small piece of hardware with physical buttons — with one button per scene, so switching is a single press. That is covered in a later lesson.

Part 6

What overlays are

His own way of putting it: the scene is the page, and the overlays are everything you place on that page.

30

What a real set-up looks like

Before the step-by-step, it is worth seeing where this ends up. This is his working profile — not a demo.

Four windows are open: Scenes, Overlays, Camera Switcher and Sound Levels. Do not be put off by how much is in there. It was built one piece at a time.

Seen on screen

31

The buttons that add overlays

Along the bottom of the Overlays window sit two rows of small buttons. Each one adds a different kind of overlay.

You do not have to guess what they do. Hover your mouse over one and wait — a small label appears telling you its name. The first one on the top row says New Image Overlay.

The lesson works along these buttons in order, which is the order this manual follows too.

Seen on screen

32

The two places an overlay can live

The Overlays list has headings that split it into sections:

  • SHOW IN ALL SCENES — this thing appears on every single scene. Use it for a logo you always want up.
  • SHOW IN CURRENT SCENE — this thing only appears on the scene you are on right now.
  • SHOW IN BACKGROUND — this sits behind everything.

When you drag something in, it lands in the current scene section by default. Switch to another scene and it is gone. That is expected, not a fault.

Part 7

Picture overlays

"Image overlay" does not just mean photos. Logos, cut-out characters and even multi-page documents all come in this way.

33

Two ways to bring a picture in

The button. Click the image button at the bottom of the Overlays window and find the file on your Mac.

Dragging. Keep a Finder window open beside Ecamm and drag files straight onto the picture. Quicker, and it is what he does throughout the lesson.

Seen on screen

34

It will come in far too big

Do not be alarmed. A large file drops in at full size and fills the screen. That is normal.

Seen on screen

35

Make it the size you want

Click the picture once to select it. A thin outline with corner marks appears.

Then either scroll with two fingers on your trackpad or mouse wheel to grow and shrink it, or drag a corner.

If clicking does nothing: images with see-through parts only respond where there is something solid. Click on a coloured part of the picture, not the empty space around it.

Seen on screen

36

Cut off the empty space around it

Look closely at the selection outline. It is often bigger than the picture itself, because the file has transparent space baked around the edges. That makes it awkward to place, and it means the overlay grabs your mouse when you are nowhere near it.

Same trick as before: hover the edge until you get the double-headed arrow, hold Option, and drag inwards. The empty space gets trimmed off rather than the picture being squashed.

Work round all four sides. Take your time — it needs a big enough grab area to catch the handle.

Seen on screen

37

Place it properly

Drag it where you want it. A faint grid appears as you move, and edges snap when they line up.

For the last bit of tidying, select the overlay and tap the arrow keys. Each tap nudges it a tiny amount. He counts his taps — ten down, ten left — so every scene gets the same gap from the edge.

A logo pushed right into the corner with no gap looks like a mistake. A small, even gap on both sides looks deliberate. That is the whole difference.

Seen on screen

38

Give a photo its own scene

Dropping a photo next to yourself in your usual scene can look cramped. A cleaner approach: make a whole scene for it.

Add a scene, name it after whatever the photo is, and set its source to Blank so you get the black canvas to build on.

Then bring in the photo, and bring yourself in as a camera overlay beside it.

Seen on screen

39

Click the thumbnail, not the name

People get stuck here and think Ecamm is broken. Clicking a scene's name gets the name ready for renaming. It does not switch you to that scene.

To actually go to a scene, click its little picture — the thumbnail on the left of the row. A white frame round the thumbnail means you are on it.

If your changes are not showing up, this is almost always why.

Seen on screen

40

Dress the photo up

Click the pencil icon on the overlay to open its settings. You get:

  • Replace Asset… — swap the file without rebuilding the overlay
  • Border and Border Width
  • Corner Radius, with a small four-square icon beside it that lets you round only some corners
  • Under More Options: Opacity, Rotation, Blend Mode, Transition and Shadow with colour, blur, angle and distance

His settings in the lesson: border 5, corners rounded at 20. The number shows on screen as you drag, so you can use the same figure everywhere.

Seen on screen

Part 8

Front, back and locking

Overlays stack. When two of them touch, one has to be on top. And once you have something exactly where you want it, you can pin it down so your own mouse cannot ruin it.

41

The list order is the stacking order

In the SHOW IN CURRENT SCENE section, the item at the top of the list sits on top in the picture.

To change which one overlaps which, drag a row up or down the list.

Seen on screen

42

Or right-click the overlay itself

Right-click an overlay in the picture and a menu opens with everything you need:

  • Lock Overlay — pin it so it cannot be moved
  • Hide Overlay
  • Copy and Paste Settings
  • Duplicate
  • Bring to Front and Send to Back
  • Reveal In Finder — jumps to the file on your Mac

Seen on screen

43

Lock it once it is right

Choose Lock Overlay. A small padlock appears beside that overlay's name in the list. It can no longer be moved or resized by accident.

If you do need to change it later, click it and Ecamm asks whether you want to unlock it. Say yes.

He says he has knocked overlays out of place mid-live more than once, and that on a client presentation it would be far worse than on a teaching session. Lock the things you have spent time positioning.

Seen on screen

44

You can lock a whole scene too

As well as locking one overlay, you can lock an entire scene. Nothing in it can then be added, moved or changed.

Handy once a scene is finished and you do not want to touch it again.

Part 9

Borders

Borders are where a screen starts looking like it belongs to you rather than to the software. There is a plain version and a newer fading version.

45

A plain coloured border

Open the overlay's settings with the pencil icon. Set Border: to Color, click the colour square, and pick your colour. Then set the thickness with Border Width.

Seen on screen

46

Save your brand colours so you stop hunting

Clicking the colour square opens the standard Mac colour window. At the bottom is an eyedropper — click it, then click anywhere on your screen to take that exact colour.

Better still, save your colours once. The colour window keeps a named list; his reads ELA LIVE, ELA LIVE 2, ELA LIVE 3. After that you pick by name instead of matching by eye every time.

Seen on screen

47

A border that fades between two colours

This one is new. Change Border: from Color to Gradient. Two colour squares appear instead of one — the border fades from the first to the second.

Pick both. If you choose the same colour twice, nothing changes — that is the point of having two.

Subtle version: pick your brand colour, then a slightly darker shade of it. You get a soft shift rather than a rainbow.

Seen on screen

48

Where the fade runs, and making it move

Beside the two colour squares there is a small round control. Drag it to set the direction of the fade — side to side, top to bottom, or diagonally from a corner.

There is also a tick box that makes the fade travel slowly round the edge of the box while you are live.

His warning, and it is a fair one: go easy. Nothing breaks, but a border chasing itself round the screen pulls attention away from you. People came to hear you, not to watch the frame.

Seen on screen

Part 10

Fun extras

Three small things that use the same picture overlay you already know.

49

A character that flies in

Drag in a cut-out picture with a see-through background — his is a cartoon of himself waving.

Open its settings with the pencil icon and set Transition: to one of the fly-in options, such as from the left.

Now use the eye icon beside its name to show and hide it. It flies in when shown and disappears when hidden.

Later on you can put that show-and-hide on a physical button, so one press brings it in and another sends it away.

Seen on screen

50

An end screen for videos

If you record videos for a channel, you normally add the "watch these next" panel afterwards in an editing app. You do not have to.

Bring the end screen graphic in as a picture overlay. Then add your camera as another overlay and use Send to Back so you sit behind the panel rather than on top of it.

Now you can talk your viewers out at the end with the panel already on screen. No editing afterwards.

Seen on screen

51

A document you can page through

A picture overlay accepts a document file, not just photos. Bring in a multi-page document and small arrows appear on it. Click them to move through the pages while you talk.

Tilt it very slightly with the Rotation slider and add a soft Shadow and it looks like a real sheet of paper sitting there.

Part 11

Moving overlays

Little animated clips — a banner that slides in, falling confetti, a cartoon of you waving. They come in exactly like pictures: drag the file on, resize, reposition. Two things about them are worth knowing before you start.

52

The sound problem, and how to fix it

This is the one that catches everybody. An animated overlay in the usual movie format plays no sound. Your confetti bursts in complete silence.

To keep the sound, convert the file to a format called WebM first. He points to a free tool, shutterencoder.com: drag your file on, choose WebM, and it converts.

If your Mac refuses to install that one, the lesson page offers handbrake.fr instead — slower, but it works.

You can spot the difference in his own overlay list: his confetti file is named Confetti_VP9 (converted, has sound) while his waving clip is me-wave.mov (silent).

Seen on screen

53

Knock the background out of a clip

If you find an animation online that has a plain green background behind it, Ecamm can remove that background so the character sits on your scene with no box round it.

Open the overlay's settings, go to More Options, and click the Blend Mode box. A long list drops down. The two at the top are the ones that matter: Green Screen and Blue Screen. Everything below them — Darken, Multiply, Colour Burn and the rest — are picture effects you can safely ignore.

Seen on screen

Part 12

Screen share in full

Part 2 used a screen share to build a comments scene. This is the same tool taught properly, with every setting visible. It is the thing he says he uses most.

54

Start a clean scene and name it

Add a scene, call it something clear — his is Screenshare — and set its source to Blank. The Overlays list under SHOW IN CURRENT SCENE is empty, which is exactly right. You are starting from nothing.

The background you put in earlier is still there under SHOW IN BACKGROUND, so you are not staring at black.

Seen on screen

54a

The old way, so you recognise it

Before the overlay existed, sharing your screen worked the way it does in a meeting app. You used a link at the top of the Ecamm window, chose what to share, and then floated your camera over it as a small picture-in-picture window.

You can still do it. It is just far less flexible — the camera sits in its own floating window with its own close button and mute control, rather than being an overlay you can size, border and layer.

He shows it once and then moves on. If you have followed older instructions and ended up with a floating camera window, this is why.

Seen on screen

54b

The little zoom control

When Zoom To App Windows is ticked, a small control appears over the shared window. It holds a miniature Show: menu, the Zoom To App Windows tick box, and a plus and minus pair.

The plus and minus zoom in and out of the window you are sharing, without opening the full settings panel. Handy mid-live when text on a page is too small for people to read.

Seen on screen

55

Bring yourself in first

Add your camera as an overlay before you add anything else. Size it and park it where you want to sit — top left works well, because that is where people look first.

Getting yourself placed first means the shared window has to fit round you, rather than the other way about.

Seen on screen

56

Add the screen share

Now click the screen share button. It drops in small, showing whatever it picked by default.

Two small round buttons appear on it: a cross to remove it, and a pencil to open its settings. The pencil is the one you want.

Seen on screen

Do not panic at what you see first. What drops in is a plain coloured box with your window sitting small inside it. The box is the frame; the window is the contents. Until you set the shape to Custom and drag the box down to match, the two will not line up. That is the whole reason the next step exists.

57

Pick the shape — and why Custom wins

Click Shape: and you get a list of fixed shapes:

  • Primary Display Aspect and Second Display Aspect — match the shape of your screens
  • Wide (16:9), Classic (4:3), Square, Circle, Squircle, Tall (9:16)
  • Custom at the bottom

The fixed shapes force your window into a box it does not fit, and you get thick empty margins. Custom lets you drag the box to whatever shape the window actually is. Use Custom.

Seen on screen

58

Choose the window, not the whole screen

By default the share picks Primary Display — your entire screen, menu bar, clutter and all.

Click Show: and choose the app instead. Apps with a small arrow beside them can be opened further to pick one particular window.

On his Mac the list reads: Current Application, ClickUp, Ecamm Live Beta, Google Chrome, Keynote. Yours will list whatever you have open.

Seen on screen

59

Green Screen Key — the trick with no green screen

Even with one window chosen, you sometimes get your desktop peeping out around the edges of it.

In More Options there is a tick box called Green Screen Key. There is no green screen anywhere — the name is borrowed. What it actually does is knock out the background sitting behind the window, so only the window itself shows.

Tick it and the leftover desktop disappears.

It has a cost. He turns it off again later, because with the background knocked out a coloured border keeps the old spacing and sits away from the window. His fix is to turn the keying off and trim the edges with Option and drag instead. Try it both ways on your own set-up.

Seen on screen

60

Decide who sits in front

Your camera box and the shared window will overlap somewhere. The one higher up the Overlays list wins.

Drag the rows to swap them, or right-click the overlay and use Bring to Front or Send to Back.

His preference is his camera slightly over the corner of the window rather than the window over him.

Seen on screen

The finished screen share scene

Camera in a bordered box at the top left, the website filling the rest, both with the same orange edge and the same rounded corners.

Now, live, you simply click this scene and carry on talking while you scroll the page.

Part 13

Slides on screen

A slide deck is just another window to share. Copy the screen share scene you already built, point it at your slides, and you have a presentation scene.

62

Copy the scene and rename it

Select your screen share scene and click the duplicate button — the second one along the bottom of the Scenes window.

Rename the copy straight away. His is called Presentation, which sits in the list under Screenshare.

You keep the camera position, the borders and the background. All you change is which window it shows.

Seen on screen

63

Point it at your slides

Open your slide app and leave it open on the desktop. In the copy's settings, change Show: from the browser to the slide app.

Your slides appear in the scene straight away.

Seen on screen

64

Click back into Ecamm before you resize

Worth repeating, because it will happen to you. After clicking into your slide app, the menu bar at the top of the screen says the slide app's name, and Ecamm's handles will not respond.

Click once on the Ecamm window. The menu bar changes back to Ecamm Live Beta. Now the handles work again.

Then hold Option and drag the edges in to trim off whatever you do not want showing.

Seen on screen

65

Move through your slides while you talk

Click into your slide app and change slides there. The scene follows along live.

You are not driving anything from inside Ecamm — you work in your slides exactly as you normally would, and the audience sees the change.

Seen on screen

66

Tilt it, if you like

Drag the Rotation slider a little. A small number appears as you drag — he uses 5 — and the slide tips slightly, like a card set down on a table.

To undo it, double-click the slider and it snaps back to zero.

A small tilt looks considered. A big one looks like something has gone wrong. Five is plenty.

Where you are up to

Look at the scene list at this point and you can see the shape of a whole show: an intro, a plain camera shot, a comments scene, a screen share, a presentation.

Every one named. Every one ready. During a live you do nothing but click down the list.

Part 14

Text on screen

What used to be one text option is now two. The difference between them decides whether lining things up is easy or maddening, so it is worth understanding before you start.

68

Find the text button

Along the bottom of the Overlays window, hover over the fourth button on the top row — the one shaped like a letter T. The label reads New Dynamic Text Overlay.

Unlike pictures, you do not drag anything in. You click this and type.

Seen on screen

69

The text window

A window called Add Text Overlay opens. Here is what is on it:

  • Style: at the top left — this is the dynamic-or-text-box choice explained in the next step
  • Three alignment buttons — left, centre, right
  • Text Color, Kerning (the gaps between letters), Line Height (the gaps between lines)
  • An Effect tick box, set to Shadow
  • Edit Background
  • Four columns: Collection, Typeface, Style, Size
  • A big black box at the bottom where you type. It starts with the word Text in it.

A line at the bottom reminds you: to add a new line, hold Shift while pressing Return.

Seen on screen

70

Group your regular fonts so you stop scrolling

The fonts offered are simply the ones installed on your Mac. Anything you download from a font website appears here once it is installed.

Click All Fonts and you get hundreds, which is no help at all when you are live. So make a collection: the + above the Collection column adds one.

His list shows collections named ELA LIVE and IMPACT — one per brand — alongside the built-in Favorites, Recently Used, Fixed Width, Fun and Modern.

Click your own collection and the Typeface column shrinks to just the two or three fonts you actually use.

Seen on screen

71

The four kinds of text — and which to use

Click the Style: box and four choices drop down. This is the whole point of the lesson, so it is worth getting straight:

  • Dynamic — drag the box bigger and the letters grow with it.
  • Text Box (Shrink to Fit) — the letters stay the size you set. If the box gets too small for them, they shrink to fit.
  • Text Box (Truncate) — same, but text that does not fit gets cut off instead of shrinking.
  • Scrolling Ticker — text that slides across, like a news band.

Why it matters. With Dynamic, the size number stops matching what you see. You set 24, you stretch the box, it still says 24 while looking twice that. Lining several pieces of text up becomes guesswork.

Which to use: Dynamic for one piece of text on its own. A text box whenever things need to line up — a list, bullet points, two banners the same size.

Seen on screen

The clearest way to see the difference is to make two pieces of the same text, set one to each style, and stretch both by the same amount. The next two pictures are exactly that.

71a

Setting the size on a text box

Because a text box will not grow its letters when you drag it, the only way to make the writing bigger is to go back in and set the number.

Click Edit Text… at the top of the settings panel. The full window opens with the four columns — Collection, Typeface, Style, Size.

The Size column has a box at the top showing the current number, a list of common sizes below it (12, 13, 14, 18, 24, 36, 48, 64) and a slider down the right. Type a number, pick one from the list, or drag the slider.

This is the trade. A text box costs you a trip back into this window every time you want the writing bigger — and in exchange it lets several pieces of text line up properly, which dynamic text will not.

Seen on screen

71b

More than one line of text

The typing box at the bottom of the window takes several lines. Pressing Return on its own will not give you one — the window tells you what to do along the bottom: hold down Shift while pressing the Return key.

Paste a list in and every line comes through. This is where Line Height earns its place: with several lines stacked, that slider sets how far apart they sit.

Beside Kerning there is a small ×. That clears the letter spacing back to normal in one click, which saves nudging the slider back by eye.

Seen on screen

72

Copy a piece of text instead of rebuilding it

To make a second piece of text with the same font, colour and styling, hold Option, then click and drag the existing one. It copies rather than moves.

The copy appears in the Overlays list as Copy of whatever the first one was called. Rename it.

This is how you compare the two styles side by side, which is exactly what he does: one Dynamic, one text box, same words.

Seen on screen

73

Letter spacing and line spacing

Two controls in the text window are newer than the rest:

  • Kerning — the gap between letters. Drag it up and the word stretches wide, each letter standing apart. That very spaced-out look is what a designer charges for; it used to be impossible here.
  • Line Height — the gap between lines. Useful when you have stacked several lines and they are sitting too close.

To add a second line, hold Shift while pressing Return. The window tells you this along the bottom.

Seen on screen

74

Backgrounds behind the text

Click the pencil icon on the text overlay and the Background: box offers several kinds. The two worth using:

  • Color — a solid block behind the words. Pick the colour with the square beside it.
  • Gradient — two colour squares, fading from one to the other. Pick your brand colour and a slightly darker version of it for a soft, expensive-looking band.

And the ones he shows but advises against: Blur softens whatever is behind the words, and Pixelate breaks it into blocks. Both are visible in the grabs below so you know what they do.

Text Margin sets how much space sits between the words and the edge of the coloured block. Too little looks cramped; too much looks like a mistake.

His advice, and he is firm about it: a plain colour or a gentle fade. Leave the blurring, the pixelating and the moving gradients alone — they look tacky and they pull attention off you.

Seen on screen

75

The shadow trick

This one is genuinely clever and worth knowing.

Set Background: to None, then tick Shadow. Because there is no block to put a shadow round, Ecamm puts it round the letters themselves.

Turn the Blur down to 0 and the shadow goes hard and crisp. That is how you make plain white text readable over a busy picture without slapping a coloured box behind it.

With a background turned on, the same setting shadows the block instead.

Seen on screen

76

Cutout text, and the rest

Under More Options there is a tick box called Cutout Text. It makes the letters see-through, so the picture shows through the words rather than round them. For it to read at all, the text has to be big.

The same panel also carries Opacity, Rotation, Blend Mode and Transition — the same controls you met on pictures, working the same way. Transition is the one worth using: it makes the text fly in from a side rather than simply appearing.

Seen on screen

Part 15

Scrolling tickers

The third text style. A band of words that slides across the screen, the way a news channel runs headlines along the bottom.

77

Turn text you already have into a ticker

You do not start a ticker from scratch. Take a piece of text you have already made, click its pencil icon, and change Style: to Scrolling Ticker. It keeps your words and starts moving them.

A speed control appears, marked with a tortoise at one end and a hare at the other. Set it to a pace someone could comfortably read.

Short text looks wrong. With only a few words, the line has to travel all the way off the screen before it can come back, so you get a long empty gap. Tickers want a proper sentence — a message, a web address, an invitation.

Seen on screen

78

Dress it like a news band

Same settings as any other text. What he recommends for a ticker:

  • Background: Gradient — a light grey fading to a darker grey reads like a real news band
  • Text Margin turned up — with no edges to the band, the margin is what gives the words room top and bottom
  • No border. A border draws a line across the top and bottom and closes off the ends, which kills the sense of the band running off the edges of the screen

To resize a ticker you cannot drag its edges — you go in and change the font size. He drops his to 24.

Seen on screen

79

Put a label on the front of it

Add a second, ordinary dynamic text overlay over the left end of the ticker — "Breaking news" or whatever suits — with a solid colour background.

Black words on a light background. He tries white on yellow first and says plainly that it does not work.

Nudge it into place with the arrow keys, one small step at a time.

Seen on screen

80

The layering rule that catches people out

This is worth knowing on its own, because it looks like a fault.

Anything in "show in all scenes" sits in front of everything in "show in current scene" — no matter what order the list is in.

So if your ticker is set to show in all scenes and your label is in the current scene, the label can be top of its own list and still end up behind. Dragging it up will not fix it.

The fix is to get both into the same section: either move the ticker down into the current scene, or move the label up into all scenes.

From the talk only

81

When a text box beats dynamic text: comments

Back to the two text styles for a moment, because this is where the difference really pays.

When you bring viewers' comments on screen, each one is a different length. With dynamic text the box swells and shrinks with every new comment, jumping about and sometimes covering your face. With a text box it stays put: long comments simply get smaller to fit.

His advice: make the box bigger than you think you need, then set the sizes. The name and the comment can be set separately — he uses about 13 for the comment.

One thing Ecamm has fixed: the viewer's profile picture used to grow with the text. It no longer does. It stays one size whatever the comment.

His summary: dynamic text for a single label down the side. Text boxes for comments, bullet points, and anything where several pieces must match.

Seen on screen

Part 16

Shapes

Brand new, and he is visibly pleased about it. A shape overlay is a plain block of colour you can put anywhere — no text, no image file. Before this existed people had to make a text overlay and turn the text invisible just to get a coloured box.

82

Add a shape

Click the shape button on the bottom row of the Overlays panel. A block drops in.

Open its settings and change the shape: Rectangle, Square, Circle, or Squircle — a square with heavily rounded corners that is neither one thing nor the other.

Everything else is the same panel you already know: colour, gradient, border, corner radius, opacity, rotation, transition, shadow.

He makes the point himself: every overlay now shares one identical settings panel. It used to be a different menu for each kind. Learn it once and it applies everywhere.

Seen on screen

One thing the pictures add that the talk did not. The shape panel has a Fill setting of its own, separate from Border. That is what makes a shape a solid block rather than an empty frame.

83

Four things to actually use it for

A panel behind bullet points. Drop a rectangle down one side, turn its Opacity down so the picture shows through, set a Transition to fly in from the left, then bring your bullet points in over it one at a time.

A colour wash over your whole picture. This is how his launch intro was built: a shape at full opacity with a gradient, laid over a blurred camera, with the title and a countdown on top.

A background you no longer need a file for. Put a shape in SHOW IN BACKGROUND instead of hunting for a coloured image to drag in.

Hiding something. Set Fill: to Blur or Pixelate and the shape hides whatever is behind it — a family photo on the wall, a name on a screen. A strength slider appears beside it.

Seen on screen

A correction to what I wrote before these pictures arrived. I had put blur and pixelate under the effects at the bottom of the panel. They are not there — they are choices in the Fill box at the top. That is a better design than I described: the shape is the mask, and Fill decides whether it paints a colour or treats the picture underneath.

84

Remember it is an overlay like any other

A shape obeys the same stacking rules. If the shape is in front of your camera, it covers you. To sit in front of a background shape, your camera overlay has to be above it in the list.

It will also rotate, take a moving gradient, and carry a shadow — all the usual controls. His warning about the moving gradient applies here too: go easy.

Part 17

Countdowns and clocks

A timer you can put on screen. He admits it is not the first thing a beginner needs, but it is behind some of the nicest things he does.

85

The five kinds

  • Count down for an amount of time — five minutes from when it starts. The one he uses most.
  • Count down to a time every day — always lands on 5pm, whenever you start.
  • Count down to a date and time — for a one-off event.
  • Clock — just the time of day. Someone asked for it so a news-style screen could have a clock in the corner.
  • Stopwatch — counts up. For timing someone.

Each type changes the field beside it: an amount of time gives you Duration, a time every day gives you End Time, and a date and time gives you a date box with a small calendar you can click through.

There is also a tick box, Show Milliseconds, which turns the hundredths on and off.

A stopwatch gets two extra buttons on the picture itself — a circular arrow to reset it and a pause button — so you can start, stop and reset it live.

A countdown is made in the same Add Text Overlay window as ordinary text — same fonts, same colours — with a Type: box added along the bottom.

Seen on screen

Worth seeing in his own set-up: open the folders in his overlay list and the countdowns are right there — 1:00 Countdown inside "Are you here?", and Countdown to 17:20 inside "Doors open" beside the text that reads GROUP OPENS IN. That second one is the count-down-to-a-time kind, doing exactly the job described below.

86

Two settings that make it run itself

  • Auto start — begins the moment you switch to that scene, so you do not have to press anything.
  • Go to next scene when finished — hands over by itself when it reaches zero.

Those two together are what makes a hands-off intro. His launch intro was a one minute ten countdown, auto-started, set to move on at zero, timed to land exactly when the music finished.

These are the same two settings as the video controller in Part 1, doing the same job.

Seen on screen

86a

Build it from parts, in a folder

His "are you here?" prompt is three separate overlays kept together in a folder, and you can watch him switch them on one at a time:

  1. A shape — the blue box with the orange edge, matching his camera frame.
  2. A text overlay reading ARE YOU HERE?
  3. A 1:00 countdown underneath it.

Because they sit in one folder, he can move all three together, and show or hide the whole thing with the folder's own eye icon.

This one has no next-scene setting. It just runs out, while he says "comment within a minute or we spin again."

Seen on screen

87

Use a fixed-width font, or the numbers jiggle

Small thing, very visible once you see it. In most fonts a 1 is narrower than a 0, so as the numbers change the whole timer shuffles sideways. It looks unsteady.

The fix: pick a font where every character is the same width. Ecamm has these grouped for you under Fixed Width in the Collection column.

The fonts in that collection on his Mac: Andale Mono, Courier New, Lantinghei TC, Menlo, Monaco, Osaka, PCMyungjo and PT Mono. Yours will be much the same — these ship with the Mac.

They are a bit plain. That matters less than a timer that sits still.

If the timer sits inside a border you will barely notice it. Sitting on its own, as a big countdown, it is obvious.

Seen on screen

88

The invisible timer trick

This is the clever one, and it is worth the price of the lesson.

Suppose you want a scrolling ticker, or your name, to show for one minute and then go away without you touching anything.

  1. Put a countdown on that scene, set to your amount of time.
  2. Tick Auto start and Go to next scene when finished.
  3. Make it big — easier to grab hold of.
  4. Then turn off its background, turn off its border, and drag the text Opacity down to zero.

The timer is still there, still counting, still going to hand over — and nobody can see it. The scene changes on its own after exactly the time you set.

His own warning. Editing one of these is awkward: the moment you switch to that scene the timer starts, and it will jump you to the next scene while you are still fiddling with it. Pause it before you edit. And take it off again when you are done, or you will spend an afternoon wondering why a scene keeps changing by itself.

Seen on screen

89

Other ways he uses them

"Be right back". On a long session, a holding screen with a five minute countdown and soft music.

"You have one minute to comment." A one minute countdown beside a prize draw, to create a bit of urgency. That one has no next-scene setting — it just runs out.

Homework or introductions. A small timer in the corner, set to move on to a scene that says "time's up".

A webinar that starts dead on time. Use count down to a date and time. Start your live a couple of minutes early and it will land on the exact minute regardless.

Part 18

Web pages in a scene

A widget overlay puts a live web page straight into your scene. No browser window open on your desktop, nothing to arrange beforehand. He says it has changed how he does screen shares, and he is right that it is the standout of this section.

90

Why it beats a screen share

A screen share needs the real window open and sitting on your desktop — awkward on a laptop, and something to remember before every live.

A widget overlay holds the address itself. Close every browser window you like; the page is still in your scene.

From the talk only

91

Add one

Click the widget button on the bottom row of the Overlays panel. A small window asks for two things:

  • Display name — what it will be called in your overlay list. Nothing more. Name it something you will recognise.
  • The web address.

Leave the size, frame rate and resolution as they come unless you have reason not to. Then click Add widget overlay.

Under Widget Options there is more: Width and Height (1280 by 720 by default), Frame Rate (30 FPS), and three tick boxes — High-Resolution Mode, Keeping Running and Fill Entire Frame — plus a Transition menu.

Then click Add Widget Overlay.

Size it, trim it with Option and drag as usual, and put your border on — his 4 and 20 again.

Start by duplicating your screen share scene and renaming the copy. You keep the camera position, the border and the layout, and only swap the shared window for the web page.

Seen on screen

92

Clicking and scrolling the page while live

By default the page just sits there and you cannot touch it.

Move your mouse over it and a small toolbar appears along the bottom. Tick the control on that toolbar and the page comes alive — you can scroll it and click its buttons exactly as if it were open in front of you.

The toolbar holds a hand icon with a switch beside it, then back, forward and reload arrows — a small set of browser controls sitting under your scene.

Lock the overlay first. Right-click it and choose Lock Overlay — a padlock appears beside its name — or it will slide around while you are trying to click things on the page.

Seen on screen

93

"Keep running" decides what happens when you leave

In the widget's settings there is a tick box called Keep running.

Ticked: you scroll down, click through to another page, switch to a different scene and come back — and it is exactly where you left it.

Not ticked: coming back reloads the original address and everything you did is undone.

Which you want depends on the job. Mid-demonstration, ticked. A page that should always open clean, not ticked.

To change it after the fact, click the pencil on the overlay, then Edit Widget… at the top of the panel — that reopens the same window with a Save button.

Seen on screen

94

The limit, and the good idea

The limit: it has to be a public web address. Anything behind a password just shows you the sign-in page. He cannot show his own course this way.

The good idea: make a folder of widget scenes for the pages you point people at again and again — your sales page, your booking page, the three videos you are always recommending. Then it is one click, live, with no typing and no hunting for a tab.

95

While we are here: save your colours

He mentions this in passing and it is worth pulling out, because it saves time on everything above.

In the colour window there are sections along the bottom holding small palettes. You can make your own: pick a colour, then hit the plus or drag the colour into the row. It is saved.

His are grouped by brand — one set for each. After that you pick your exact colour by clicking it, rather than matching by eye every time.

The menu at the top of the colour window lists them all. On his Mac: Web Safe Colours, Crayons, IMPACT branding, ELA LIVE, Developer and Apple. The middle two are his own.

Pick one and you get just those colours, listed by name. The + under the search box adds the colour you are on; the − removes one.

His one grumble: getting rid of a saved colour afterwards is harder than adding one.
